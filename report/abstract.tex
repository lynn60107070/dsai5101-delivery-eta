\begin{abstract}
Food delivery platforms promise customers a delivery time the moment an order is placed, before a
courier is assigned. We ask how accurately the delivery time can be predicted at that moment, using only
information known then, and which order-time conditions drive long deliveries. Using eight days of
public Meituan data (546{,}340 orders after cleaning), we train Elastic-Net, Random Forest and gradient
boosting models on six days with forward-chaining cross-validation and score them once on the two
held-out days. Gradient boosting predicts delivery time with a mean absolute error of 6.49 minutes,
31\% lower than a median predictor (9.46) and 3.1 minutes lower than the platform's own promised time
(9.62), with all differences confirmed by paired bootstrap intervals. Random Forest (6.54) is only
0.05 minutes behind, which suggests that accuracy is limited by the information available at ordering
rather than by the model. Distance is by far the strongest driver, ahead of time of day, district and
estimated preparation time, while recent district load matters little. Turning predictions into
promises is a different matter: at the same average promised time (38.2 minutes), an 80th-percentile
model is late slightly more often than the platform (6.1\% vs 5.0\% of orders more than five minutes
late), likely because the platform can still act after the order is placed. Order-time prediction
therefore gives clearly better estimates, but not better promises on its own.
\end{abstract}
